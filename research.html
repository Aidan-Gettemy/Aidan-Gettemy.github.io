Research

Much of what we would like to know is hard to observe directly: the rock far beneath the surface, the wind a turbine is really facing, the condition of a blade high above the ground. What we have instead is indirect information, such as a handful of pressure readings or a stream of sensor data, together with a physics-based model that connects the two. My research is about working backward from that information to what we actually care about, and being honest about how certain we can be. The thread running through the three projects below is high-dimensional, physics-based problems, with the goal of uncertainty quantification and parameter identification.

Bayesian inverse problems

Manuscript in preparation

Permeability, a measure of how easily fluid moves through rock, cannot be measured everywhere underground, so it has to be inferred from limited pressure data. A Bayesian approach does this by returning not a single best guess but a whole distribution of plausible permeability fields that match the data, which is what lets us say how uncertain the answer is. This is joint work with Felipe Pereira at UT Dallas, Marcio Borges at the Laboratório Nacional de Computação Científica, and Rafael Guiraldello.

In our setup, the log-permeability is modeled as a Gaussian random field in two or three spatial dimensions, and the coefficients θ of its Karhunen–Loève expansion become the unknowns to infer. We place a hierarchical prior on top, with two additional parameters: β, which controls the overall level of the field, and ρ, which controls how heterogeneous it is. Standard MCMC methods such as preconditioned Crank–Nicolson (pCN) become very slow in this setting. The reason, as we first recognized, is severe non-uniqueness: many combinations of θ, β, and ρ produce identical simulated data, so the chains have no way to settle.

Our remedy uses one extra measurement, the effective permeability of the entire sample. Combined with an offline curve built by Monte Carlo sampling, it gives an empirical relationship between β and a function of ρ. Eliminating one parameter removes the non-uniqueness that was hampering convergence of the multi-chain R-hat metric, and it makes efficient sampling possible.

My part was largely computational. I ran the experiments on UT Dallas’s high-performance computing clusters, ganymede2 and Juno, using SLURM and MPI to simulate the Markov chains. I then analyzed the results: convergence through R-hat, coverage, reconstruction error, and the posterior fields themselves. This project is part of a larger body of work on domain-decomposed MCMC for porous media problems, and I will add the rest here as it is completed.

Surrogate modeling

Wind Energy Science, 2026

Erosion on the leading edge of a wind turbine blade degrades performance, and catching it early matters. A system that monitors blades continuously from time-series sensor data has to be built on a large, comprehensive data set that covers many loading conditions and eroded states. Generating that data by repeatedly running a full physics-based simulator quickly becomes impractical, and that is where a surrogate model comes in: a fast stand-in for a slow simulator. This work is joint with Susan Minkoff, John Zweck, and Elaine Spiller.

We trained a multi-dimensional emulator on a carefully selected set of ground-truth simulations. Once trained, it is essentially free to run, producing new samples for a new input setting almost instantly. To test it, we trained two competing classifiers: one on full simulation data and the other purely on emulated data. The well-trained emulator performed as well as the full simulator on this task, while taking only 1/10,000 of the time to produce a new data point.

I simulated turbine behavior under varying inflow conditions and erosion severity with OpenFAST, and wrote a new MATLAB framework to automate those simulations and the processing of their output. I then evaluated supervised learning and Gaussian process emulator models for detecting erosion. We see this as a step toward a full digital twin for condition monitoring.

Nacelle transfer function (NTF) modeling

Manuscript under review

Wake steering deliberately turns an upstream turbine slightly out of the wind to improve the output of the turbines behind it. But a turbine that is yawed this way also reads the wind differently, so its own sensors become biased, which makes it hard to tell whether the steering is really helping. Nacelle transfer functions (NTFs) are the tools used to correct wind measurements taken at the nacelle, and our work asks what they look like for steered turbines. This is joint work with Luke Abbatessa and Nathan Post.

Operational SCADA data records quantities such as nacelle heading, wind speed, and power generation. To evaluate how steering changes the power of an individual turbine, the changes in these sensor measurements need to be read without bias from the steering itself. We filtered out implausible data and used consensus estimation to calculate what a turbine would measure while facing the wind its neighbors see. We then trained a machine learning model, on intervals when turbines were aligned with the wind, to predict the true sensor measurement from those consensus values. With that model we can predict the difference between the aligned sensor values and the actual, biased measurements during the intervals when steering is active. Our analysis and uncertainty quantification found signs of sensor bias during steering. They also revealed large spatio-temporal effects across the farm that lower the signal-to-noise ratio of the data, which complicates the analysis of bias itself.

I built the Python data pipelines and filtering tools, developed the consensus and machine learning models, and presented the results at NAWEA/WindTech 2025.
