Research

The unifying theme across my research projects is the challenge of high-dimensional, physics-based problems, where the goal is to quantify uncertainty and identify parameters.

Bayesian inverse problems

With Felipe Pereira (University of Texas at Dallas), Marcio Borges (Laboratório Nacional de Computação Científica), and Rafael Guiraldello · Paper in preparation

Subsurface properties such as permeability cannot be measured everywhere, so they must be inferred from limited pressure data. In the Bayesian approach, we place a Gaussian random field prior on the log-permeability field in two or three spatial dimensions. We represent the discretized field with a Karhunen–Loève expansion and treat its coefficients (θ) as the unknown parameters. The Bayesian inversion then returns a distribution of plausible permeability fields that match the data. Sampling that distribution with standard Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, such as preconditioned Crank–Nicolson (pCN), becomes very slow, however, when the prior is hierarchical. A hierarchical prior adds two more parameters: β, which sets the overall level of the field, and ρ, which controls how heterogeneous it is.

Our goal was stable, efficient MCMC sampling for the hierarchical model. The obstacle was severe non-uniqueness, since several combinations of θ, β, and ρ lead to identical simulated data, so we sought a construction that removes it.

Using one additional measurement, the effective permeability of the entire sample, together with an offline curve built by Monte Carlo sampling, we construct an empirical relationship between β and a function of ρ. This lets us eliminate one parameter, which removes the non-uniqueness that was hampering convergence of the multi-chain R-hat metric (the potential scale reduction factor) and allows for efficient sampling.

We tested the method by simulating dozens of Markov chains on HPC infrastructure, examining whether independent chains converged to the same answer, how well the results covered the true values, how closely the reconstructed fields matched the truth, and what the posterior fields themselves looked like.

Surrogate modeling

With Susan Minkoff, John Zweck, and Elaine Spiller · Wind Energy Science, 2026

Erosion on the leading edge of a wind turbine blade degrades performance and, if it progresses unchecked, leads to expensive repairs. Catching it early calls for continuous condition monitoring, and a machine-learning-based monitoring system must learn from time-series sensor data that covers many loading conditions and eroded states. Generating that data by repeatedly running the full, physics-based simulator, however, quickly becomes impractical.

Our goal was to make this data inexpensive to produce without sacrificing accuracy. We therefore took a surrogate-model-based approach: a multi-dimensional emulator is trained on a carefully selected set of ground-truth simulations, and once trained, it generates new samples for a new input setting at essentially no cost.

We generated the ground-truth simulations with OpenFAST under varying inflow conditions and erosion severity, and a new MATLAB framework automated the simulations and the processing of their output. To validate the emulator, we trained two competing classifiers to detect erosion, one on full simulation data and the other purely on emulated data. The well-trained emulator performed as well as the full simulator at this task, while taking several orders of magnitude less time to produce a new data point. We see this as a step toward using emulators to build a full digital twin for condition monitoring.

Nacelle transfer function (NTF) modeling

With L. Abbatessa and Nathan Post · Under review

Wake steering deliberately yaws an upstream turbine to improve the output of the turbines behind it. To assess wake steering performance, the power generation of each affected turbine has to be compared with and without steering, using operational supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) data, which records measurements such as nacelle heading, wind speed, and power generation. The difficulty is that the steering itself biases a turbine's own sensors, so changes in the measurements must be separated from this bias before they can be trusted.

Our goal was to evaluate nacelle transfer functions for yawed turbines from SCADA data, and to understand the biases that occur due to the geometry of the sensor placement and the asymmetric flow induced by the rotor during steering. The key idea is to estimate what turbine would have measured without steering, so that its actual measurements can be compared against that reference.

We first filtered out implausible data and used consensus estimation to calculate the sensor measurements a turbine would see facing the wind from its neighbors. A machine learning model, trained on intervals when turbines were aligned with the wind, then learned to predict the true sensor measurement from those consensus values. Applying it during the intervals when wake steering is active lets us predict the aligned sensor values and compare them with the actual, biased measurements. Our analysis and uncertainty quantification found signs of sensor bias during steering. They also revealed large spatio-temporal effects across the farm that affect the signal-to-noise ratio of the data, which complicates the analysis of bias itself.
