Research

One unifying theme in my research projects is working with high-dimensional physics based problems with the goal to perform uncertainty quantification and parameter identification.

Bayesian inverse problems

With Felipe Pereira (University of Texas at Dallas), Marcio Borges (Laboratório Nacional de Computação Científica), and Rafael Guiraldello · Paper in preparation

The problem. Subsurface properties such as permeability cannot be measured everywhere, so they are infered from limited pressure data. In the Bayesian approach, a Gaussian random field prior is used for the log-permeability field in 2 or 3 spatial dimensions. Using the coefficients (θ) of the Karhunen–Loève expansions discretized random field as the stochastic parameter, the Bayesian inversion then returns a distribution of plausible fields that match the data. However, sampling that distribution with standard MCMC methods, such as pCN, becomes very slow when the field follows a hierarchical distribution, with two additonal parameters; (β) which controls the field level and (ρ) which controls the heterogeneity.

Our goal. To build an efficient MCMC method, we first recognized that the heirarchical prior suffered from severe non-uniqueness, where several combinations of parameters θ, β, and ρ led to identical simulated data. We sought a construction to alleviate this lack of uniquness and recover stable MCMC sampling.

Our approach. We use one additional measurement, the effective permeability of the entire sample, along with an offline curve constructed by Monte Carlo sampling to construct an empirical curve that relates β to a function of ρ. Eliminating one parameter removes the lack of uniquness, which hampers convergence of the multi-chain R-hat metric, and allows for efficient sampling. I ran experiments on the University of Texas GanymedeII and Juno machines (SLURM, MPI) to simulate the Markov chains and analyze postserior metrics, including convergence with R-hat, coverage, reconstruction error, and the posterior fields themselves.

Surrogate modeling

With Susan Minkoff, John Zweck, and Elaine Spiller · Wind Energy Science, 2026

The problem. Erosion on the leading edge of a wind turbine blade degrades performance, and catching it early matters. Designing a machine-learning based system for continuous condition monitoring by time-series sensor outputs requires a large, comprehensive data set in order to ensure accuracy under different loading conditions and eroded states. Repeatedly quuerying the full, physics-based simulator quickly becomes impractical. Thus, we adopt a surrogate model based approach.

Our goal. We train a multi-dimensional emulator on a carefully selected set of ground-truth simulations. Then, once trained, the emulator is essentially free to generate new samples given a new input setting. We validate this approach by training two competing classifiers; one trained on full simulation data and another purely on emulated data. We find that the well-trained emulator performs as well as the full simulator at this task, despite taking only 1/10,000 the time to predict a new data point.

Our approach. We simulated turbine behavior under varying inflow conditions and erosion severity with OpenFAST. To assist this task, I wrote a new MATLAB framework to automate the simulations and the processing of their output. I then evaluated supervised learning and Gaussian process emulator models for detecting erosion. We envision this as a step toward using emulators in building a full digital-twin for condition monitoring.

NTF modeling

With L. Abbatessa and Nathan Post · Under review

The problem. Wake steering deliberately yaws an upstream turbine to improve the output of the turbines behind it. Operational SCADA data records measurements such as nacelle heading, wind speed, and power generation. In order to evaluate the changes to power generation to the individual turbines effected by a wake steering move, the changes to sensor measurements need to be able to be read without bias from the steering itself.

Our goal. To evaluate yawed turbine transfer functions from SCADA data, and to understand the biases that creep in when comparing steered and unsteered turbines.

Our approach. We used filtering to remove implausible data and consensus estimation methods to calculate the sensor measurements that a turbine would see facing the wind from that turbines neighbors. We train a machine learning model to predict the true sensor measurement during time intervals where the turbines are steered into the wind from the consensus values. With this model, we can then predict the difference between aligned sensor values (from the model) and actual measurements (with bias) during the intervals where the wake steering is active. Our analysis and uncertainty quantification results found signs of sensor bias during steering, but also identified the large spatio-temporal effects across the farm that impact the signal to noise ratio in the data, complicating the analysis of bias itself. I presented the results at NAWEA/WindTech 2025.
